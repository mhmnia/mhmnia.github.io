Common-mode filtering for InSAR time series

An atmospheric correction built for the volcanoes that global weather models cannot see: small, steep edifices where the deformation signal and the atmospheric noise occupy the same few square kilometres.

Volcano InSAR has a scale problem. The deformation we care about at a small stratovolcano is a few millimetres per year over a summit maybe five kilometres across. The atmospheric phase delay sitting on top of it is often larger, varies from acquisition to acquisition, and correlates strongly with topography — which is exactly the shape of the thing we are trying to measure.

Why the usual corrections fall short

The standard fix is to model the atmosphere from an external source: a global weather reanalysis such as ERA5, or a product such as GACOS. These work well for large deformation fields. They struggle at small volcanoes for a simple reason of resolution. A weather model grid cell spans tens of kilometres; a volcano with a footprint under roughly 20 km² sits inside a single cell, or straddles two. The model has no information at the scale of the edifice, so the correction it supplies is close to a constant across the whole target — it removes the regional trend and leaves the local delay untouched.

Stacking more interferograms helps, but slowly, and it costs exactly the thing that matters most for hazard: the ability to see a transient while it is still happening.

The idea behind CMF

CMF gives up on modelling the atmosphere and measures it instead, from the data itself.

The assumption is narrow and testable. Pixels immediately surrounding a volcano share most of the atmospheric delay that the volcano experiences, but they are not deforming. So the phase they carry, once you have removed any long-wavelength ramp, is a direct sample of the noise — the common mode — at that acquisition date. Subtract it from the summit and what is left is deformation plus a much smaller residual.

  1. Define a target and a reference ring. The target is the deforming area of interest. The reference is an annulus of stable ground around it, close enough to share the same weather, far enough not to include the deformation.
  2. Estimate the common mode per date. For each acquisition, take a robust average of the reference-ring phase after removing the orbital and long-wavelength components.
  3. Subtract it from the target time series. The common mode is removed epoch by epoch, so the correction is time-varying, not a single spatial filter applied once.
  4. Check what you removed. Compare the corrected and uncorrected series, and test that the residual scatter has dropped while any transient survives. A correction that flattens the signal along with the noise has been applied over too wide a ring.

What it bought at Taftan

Taftan is the case the method was built for: a 3,900 m edifice on the Makran arc with a small summit footprint, no ground instrumentation, and no dated eruption for roughly 700,000 years. In the uncorrected time series the signal is not convincing. After filtering, a coherent ten-month episode appears — about 9 cm of uplift, peaking near 11 cm/yr, with the flanks moving gently outward.

That pattern is what shallow gas or hot fluid injection looks like. Bayesian source inversion places the source 490–630 m beneath the summit, and hyperbolic-tangent transient modelling pins down when it started and when it stopped. Nothing triggered it: no earthquake, no eruption. The full argument is in the paper.

Where CMF helps, and where it does not

Use it when the target is small relative to a weather-model grid cell, stable ground surrounds it at a similar elevation, and you are chasing a transient rather than a long-term trend.

Be careful when the deformation field is broad enough to reach into the reference ring — the filter will then remove part of your signal — or when the surrounding terrain differs sharply in elevation or land cover from the target, so the delay it samples is not the delay the target sees.

Getting the code

CMF is written in Python and released under GPL-3.0. It is built to sit inside an existing time-series workflow rather than replace one, and has been used alongside MintPy, StaMPS and LiCSBAS. It is the standard filtering step in the Tibesti province study as well.
