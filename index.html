What I actually do

A radar satellite passes over the same patch of ground every twelve days and measures the distance to it. Compare two passes and you can see the ground move. The catch is that most of what you see is not ground movement — it is water vapour in the atmosphere, changing between the two dates and mimicking exactly the signal you are hunting.

Most of my work is about telling those two apart. The common-mode filtering method I developed measures the atmospheric noise directly from stable ground around a volcano instead of modelling it, which matters at small, steep edifices where global weather models are far too coarse to help. It cuts the scatter in a time series by about a third.

Once the signal is clean, the question becomes what produced it. I use Bayesian source inversion to locate the pressure change, and thermodynamic and petrological models to work out whether a reservoir is filling, cooling, or simply venting gas. The same toolkit works away from volcanoes too: salt rising through a desert basin, an aquifer compacting under a plain, a glacier thinning above the Arctic Circle.

The reason any of this matters: most of the world's volcanoes have no seismometer, no GNSS station, no observatory. Satellites are the only instrument they will ever have.
