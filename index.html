What drives my research

The Earth is full of mysteries. Magma moves beneath volcanoes, glaciers flow, faults accumulate strain, landslides evolve, and salt rises through the Earth — but we cannot see most of these processes directly. Satellites have changed that. They are our eyes from space, and InSAR is one of the most powerful radar eyes we have. It allows us to detect tiny changes in the Earth's surface and use them to understand what is happening beneath it — from volcanoes and earthquakes to landslides, glaciers, salt domes, and subsidence.

For me, working with InSAR is much more than a technical job. It is a way of satisfying my curiosity about how the Earth works. I enjoy looking at a deformation signal and asking: What is causing it? What is happening beneath the surface? My current focus is mainly on volcano monitoring with InSAR, but I have also worked on salt-dome deformation. I am interested in any process where surface deformation can reveal something about the hidden physics of the Earth.

I particularly enjoy combining InSAR with geodetic, thermodynamic, and petrological modelling to move from simply detecting deformation to understanding its physical cause. I am also interested in improving InSAR itself. In our work on Taftan volcano, we proposed a Common-Mode Filtering (CMF) method to reduce tropospheric atmospheric errors in small-scale InSAR studies and better resolve the timing and magnitude of transient deformation.

I want to keep exploring these connections — between deformation, volcanism, earthquakes, rainfall, and eventually AI. What I enjoy most is solving a scientific question, discovering something new, and turning that discovery into a good scientific paper. And honestly, if you enjoy sitting for hours talking about volcanoes, InSAR, geophysics, or AI, I will always enjoy that conversation.
