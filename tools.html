Software and tools

Two things I have built and released openly: a method that makes small-volcano InSAR workable, and a browser tool that turns an earthquake into a shortlist of volcanoes worth checking. Both are free to use and free to reuse.

Peer-reviewed method · Python · GPL-3.0

Common-Mode Filtering for InSAR time series

Volcano InSAR has a scale problem. The deformation at a small stratovolcano is a few millimetres a year over a summit maybe five kilometres across, and the atmospheric delay sitting on top of it is often larger and correlates with topography — the exact shape of the thing being measured.

The standard fix is to model the atmosphere from a global weather reanalysis such as ERA5, or a product like GACOS. Those work well over large deformation fields and struggle at small volcanoes for a simple reason: a weather-model grid cell spans tens of kilometres, so an edifice under roughly 20 km² sits inside one cell. The correction it supplies is close to a constant across the whole target.

CMF gives up on modelling the atmosphere and measures it instead. Pixels in a ring of stable ground around the volcano share most of the same delay but are not deforming, so their phase is a direct sample of the noise at each acquisition date. Subtract that common mode epoch by epoch and the time-series scatter drops by about 36% on its own, or up to 50% alongside an ERA5 correction.

It is built to sit inside an existing workflow rather than replace one, and has been used with MintPy, StaMPS and LiCSBAS. Be careful where the deformation field is broad enough to reach into the reference ring, because then the filter removes part of your signal.

Earthquake–Volcano Proximity Explorer

After a large earthquake, which volcanoes are close enough to be worth a look? Pick a time window and a magnitude threshold, load the USGS catalogue, and click any event. The tool lists every Smithsonian GVP Holocene volcano inside your chosen radius with its distance, azimuth, last known eruption and activity evidence, drawn both as a schematic proximity plot and on a satellite basemap.

Click one of those volcanoes and it goes a step further: it queries the ASF radar archive for that point and reports which Sentinel-1, NISAR or ALOS PALSAR tracks cover it, how often each repeats, and the shortest interferogram on each track that spans the earthquake. That last number tells you whether a co-event pair exists at all before you commit to processing anything.

The volcano catalogue ships with the page and loads on its own, so there is nothing to upload before you start. Nothing you load is sent to a server. Proximity is spatial context only — a volcano inside the radius does not by itself imply the earthquake triggered anything there.
